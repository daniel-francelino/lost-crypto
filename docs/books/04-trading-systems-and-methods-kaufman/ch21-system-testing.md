## 1. Chapter summary

| ID | Concept | Problem it addresses | Pages |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | **Testing is a fight between computing power and common sense.** Better tools do not mean better results. **Optimization is the most misused technique**: "the best test results never live up to their expectations and are often a financial tragedy when traded"; everyone using a computer for testing is guilty of overfitting at some time. | Overfitting by search. | 905–906 |
| C2 | **Expectations first**: define the idea, the time frame, the relative risk and the expected percentage of winners **before** testing; otherwise one cannot tell a good idea from overfitting. **"Testing should be used for deciding between trade-offs of risk and reward, but not for discovery."** Define the **objective** (profit alone yields one or two huge winners and many small losses: gold lost for 20 years then rallied; the 1990s bull market can hide later losses); the industry favours the Sharpe/information ratio; also maximum drawdown and profit per trade (costs). | Objective and plan before search. | 907–908 |
| C3 | **Parameters and ranges**: examples of parameters (period, smoothing constant, band width, RSI period, stop, maximum holding period). 1-, 2- and n-parameter optimisations; 250×30×30 = 225,000 tests at 0.01 s = about 36 minutes; "limiting the test forces a decision, in advance, about the range of parameters that are reasonable; without these restrictions, the test moves closer to indiscriminate exploration." | Combinatorial size and pre-registered ranges. | 908–909 |
| C4 | **Distribution of tested values**: do not space periods evenly; the difference between 49 and 50 days is 2% but between 2 and 3 is 50%; use all small values and fewer large ones, or **percentage increments (multiply by 1.5 each step)**, which also shortens the test and improves the distribution of results. | Even steps overweight the long end. | 909 |
| C5 | **Order of testing**: test the most important component first (trend period), then the timing indicator, then the stop-loss; "it is unrealistic to think that a strategy should be driven by the size of the stop-loss". **Variable types**: continuous, discrete, and **coded (rule/regime) parameters**; pattern analysis and averaging make sense only for continuous/discrete variables, not for coded ones (single MA vs double MA vs regression). | Interpretable search order. | 909–910 |
| C6 | **Data**: continuous futures series (nearest, back-adjusted, indexed, constant-n-day); back-adjusted data fails percentage-based rules and can go negative; the best series is the one actually traded; keep a second unadjusted series for true highs/lows. **Synthetic data**: shuffling segments (Monte Carlo of blocks) was abandoned because it separates a bull run from its inevitable decline; random-number series look like prices in sideways periods but lack real runs, seasonality and changing volatility; model-based synthetic data (Wolberg's kernel regression, "Fraction Same Sign") is proposed; a strategy found on synthetic data may exploit the generator. | Data and synthetic data. | 910–916 |
| C7 | **Testing integrity**: in-sample about **50%** of data, rest out-of-sample (first half, last half, alternating blocks, or **alternating random blocks fixed at the start** with a random length per block, e.g. 2 to 8 years); evaluate **daily returns, not total profits**, when blocks are alternating; "in-sample data can be tortured without mercy; out-of-sample is used **once**"; typical out-of-sample result is about 50% of in-sample returns, and an information ratio dropping 2.0 → 1.0 is "reasonably good"; fixing the system after seeing out-of-sample data is **feedback** ("always overfitting"). | The discipline of the hold-out. | 916–917 |
| C8 | **Step-forward (walk-forward) testing**: choose parameters on a window (example 2 years), apply to the next 6 months, roll forward by 6 months, accumulate only out-of-sample results. **Short-term bias**: short windows favour fast systems; sign: the optimum jumps between short and long periods in successive windows (10, 50, 15…); cure: longer windows (5 years). **Feedback** again: repeated step-forward runs consume the out-of-sample data, so a final untouched data set is needed. | Walk-forward design and its biases. | 918–919 |
| C9 | **Searching for the best result**: the best is the **most robust** parameter set, not the maximum. **Sequential optimisation** (one parameter at a time) reduces tests from n1×n2×n3 to n1+n2+n3 but can miss the peak (the book's 2-D map example finds 300 instead of 400); fix with random **seeding** (scan row/column from random starts; the same answer from several seeds suggests the peak); steepest descent for continuous surfaces; automatic optimisation only makes sense if results are continuous. | Efficient search without being fooled. | 919–922 |
| C10 | **Visualising**: look at the price chart (data errors) and at the **parameter map**; 1-D patterns: a broad profitable plateau (good), two plateaus (fast vs slow; choose the slow for cost and error reasons), a narrow spike surrounded by losses (probably a short-lived shock; also high profits without costs are suspect); 2-D tables, surface plots, scatter plots by parameter; **erratic patterns mean the strategy is unreliable or the test has errors**; the stop-loss value matters less than the trend period; "if averages are the same, the value with the smallest standard deviation of profit factor shows the greatest stability". | See robustness, not just the maximum. | 922–929 |
| C11 | **Standardise results** to compare systems: annualise, risk-adjust (profit/risk ≈ information ratio), and **adjust for standard error** (two trades vs 50 trades at 50% winners are not equally reliable). **Average the neighbours** (9-box average of the map, edge versions with 6 and 4 boxes) to smooth spikes. | Comparability. | 930–932 |
| C12 | **Large-scale search**: 8 parameters with ranges can give **2,332,800,000** combinations (more than 7 years at 10 tests/s). **Genetic algorithm** recipe: pools of 1,000–5,000 strings; propagation; mating (10–15%); mutation (cell in 10% of strings); stop on convergence; **repeat up to 5 times with different seeds**; save the **top 20 strings** — if they cluster the result is more robust, if they differ the integrity is questionable. **Monte Carlo sampling** of parameter sets (1,000 random strings; check each value is picked about equally often; repeat several times); unlike the genetic algorithm it also gives the **depth** of success needed to judge robustness, and avoids targeting values known to perform well. | Search when the grid is too big, without losing robustness information. | 932–936 |
| C13 | **Refining rules**: a new rule that only turns one specific loss into a profit raises the peak but turns neighbouring parameter values into larger losses; a **very high kurtosis** of the parameter-performance distribution is a sign of overfitting; a good change improves all cases ("rule fitting that affects one or two cases is unlikely to work in the future"). | Rule patching as overfitting. | 937–938 |
| C14 | **Valid results**: "we accept good results but look closely for errors when results are bad — verify all results carefully"; a strategy tuned on a single event (the 1987 crash) is not general; a long bull market biases parameters; **omissions**: no price shock in the test data means you cannot know if you would survive one; data skewed in one direction (equities in the 1990s, bonds from the 1990s) creates biased strategies. | Conscious bias. | 938–939 |
| C15 | **Robustness criteria**: (1) a **sound premise** (each rule capitalises on a real phenomenon); (2) **adapts** to changing conditions (volatility-adjusted stops, size based on risk, no fixed price levels/dollars); (3) **tested properly** (most data, in/out of sample, or paper trading until enough trades). Futures Truth finds the best systems have **four or fewer variables**. "A robust system performs consistently with the fewest parameters tested over the most data." | What a robust system looks like. | 939–940 |
| C16 | **Benchmarks** (the 20% return versus the 25% average return); published benchmarks have **survivorship bias** and asymmetric returns (−50% then +50% is −25%). **Fix the test range of each parameter before testing** (RSI 3–10, not 3–50; if the 45-day RSI wins, the concept is probably overfitted). | Honest comparison. | 940–941 |
| C17 | **Performance criteria** (ten): profit factor/return ratio; net profit; **number of trades** (few trades are unreliable); **percentage profitable** (trend systems 30–40% is normal; >60% means regular small profits); **average net return per trade** vs costs (a $50 average per currency trade is overwhelmed by about $100 slippage); **maximum drawdown** (erratic, not the largest to come; a surprisingly small one signals overfitting or a short test; prefer the **average of the maximum drawdowns across tests**; capital is typically three times it); annualised return; **time to recovery**; **time in market** (less exposure to shocks is better at equal performance); **slope of periodic returns** (declining slope means decay). Slippage figures quoted are futures-specific. | What to report. | 941–942 |
| C18 | **Judging by the average, not the best**: expect the **average of all tests**; `adjusted return = average of all tests − 1 standard deviation of all test returns` (84% probability of achieving it under normality). **Review outliers**: results should not rest on a single major move, especially a price shock (50% chance of the same profit being a loss); study the largest wins and losses; "a number of large profits and no large losses means overfitting". | Realistic expectation. | 943 |
| C19 | **Sensitivity testing**: shift parameters (singly and jointly) by small amounts; performance should be modestly sensitive; if every neighbour is worse, the choice is a **peak solution (overfit)**; **n-space**: group tests by distance from the core in concentric shells and look at the decay of average performance per shell. | Local stability. | 943–944 |
| C20 | **Evidence discipline**: do not accept "proof" by chosen examples; "the significance of significant": the number of trials across **all** strategies tried matters, not only the current one; 70% of tests being profitable is a rule of thumb for robustness only before narrowing the range; **cross-market check**: a system profitable on the S&P but not on NASDAQ is probably overfit; the sister markets are out-of-sample data; advertised single-market systems are "too good to be true". | Multiple testing and replication. | 944–945 |
| C21 | **Comparison example** (NASDAQ 100, 1997–2003, $125 per trade): moving average vs regression slope, periods 10–250 step 10: most tests are profitable (confirming the premise); the number of trades falls in an orderly way and then stalls at ~8; closed-trade profit factor jumps misleadingly; use a daily-return information ratio; the more robust system has the higher **average** ratio over a reasonable range (2.62 vs 2.30). | Applying the rules to compare two systems. | 946–949 |
| C22 | **Costs**: NASDAQ 1991–2011 moving average with no cost, $50, $100, $150 round-turn: profitable region narrows from periods above 15 days to about 45–75 as costs rise; "costs that are too high make everything a loss; realistic costs are necessary". | Cost sensitivity curve. | 949 |
| C23 | **Worst results are information**: profit from a shock by one parameter is not a reason to choose it; a persistent loss region (periods 1–11 lose on NASDAQ because of noise) can be turned into a **fade of the fast trend inside a profitable slow trend**: *buy when the slow average is up and the fast turns down; sell when the slow is down and the fast turns up; exit on the slow turning* — net profit 64,385 → 53,380, profit factor 1.51 → 2.26, trades 212 → 72, % profitable 35 → 47 (single market). | Using the losing region. | 950–951 |
| C24 | **Retesting**: after development, one final test on all data; parameters should change only slightly; retest after **5–10% new data**; a large shift means unusual volatility or too-short original data; **after a price shock, do not make rules to fix it**; **is the model broken?**: compare the size and duration of live drawdowns with the tested profile; steady losses up to the old maximum drawdown signal a problem; probability of a drawdown ≥ P under normal returns: `Prob = (1 − P)^(2μ/σ²)` (their example: 30% yearly return, μ/σ = 0.09 daily; exponent 23.51; 95% confidence → a 12% drawdown is expected in about 5% of months); assumes normal returns (fat tails make it optimistic). | Parameter drift and live monitoring. | 951–953 |
| C25 | **Wide market testing**: the same rules over many markets is the basis for robustness; "loose pants fit everyone" (Tropin) — expect suboptimal but consistent results. | Cross-asset consistency. | 954 |
| C26 | **Price shocks**: a shock is an event that traders did not anticipate; since shocks usually go against crowded positions, "unless you are a contrarian, a price shock is more likely to cause a loss than a profit". Historical tests always contain shocks the optimiser may have profited from; with 10 shocks in 20 years, profiting from 8 means at least ~3 profits that should have been losses; erroneously low risk "allows over-leveraging"; test: remove profit/loss from 1-day moves whose range exceeds **5× the previous 10-day ATR** — trend performance consistently falls (S&P 1991–2009, Figure 21.21); average the neighbouring tests but **use the largest drawdown**; make a list of shocks (range ≥ 3.5× recent average, confirmed on charts), count wins vs losses (more than 50% wins means both profit and risk are wrong); **reverse** the average 1-day profit into the equivalent loss for those days; wins and losses should be of comparable size across shock sizes. | Shock-adjusted backtests. | 970–972 |
| C27 | **Anatomy of an optimisation** (crude oil, 1990–91): the best moving average on data ending before each shock held the wrong position on the shock day (short before the invasion; long before the US attack); the best parameter with both shocks sits between two losses (unstable) and most of its profit came from the two shocks (profits without shocks: $3,225 and $2,495 compared with $16,565–$28,335 including them); a reasonable choice would be near 60 days with far lower profits. | A real case of parameters tuned to shocks. | 972–975 |
| C28 | **Summarising robustness**: after in-sample success and good out-of-sample, retest with all data and start small; robust = good across many parameter values, many markets, many years; judge by **averages** (net profit, trades, drawdown, profit per trade) and their **standard deviation**, not by the best; checklist item 1: validate an existing idea (sound premise: a pattern in human behaviour or an economic/fundamental process). | Final definition. | 976 |

## 3. Opportunities

Grouped; the first group is cheap and high value.

### O1 — Sweep robustness report: aggregates, neighbourhood and plateau *(book: C10, C11, C18, C19, C28, pp. 922–932, 943–944, 976)*
* **Proposal:** for every sweep, in addition to the ranking, compute: mean, median, standard deviation, percentage of profitable runs (in net return and in return after costs), the **adjusted return = mean − 1σ**, the **neighbourhood average** of each run (9-box average in 2-D, edge versions at borders; mean of the two neighbours in 1-D), a **sensitivity score** of the selected run (how much the neighbour average decays), the **average maximum drawdown** and the **largest** maximum drawdown across neighbours, and a flag **"peak solution"** when all neighbours are worse than the selected run. In the app show a 1-D curve or 2-D heatmap, and warn on erratic maps (the book: erratic means unreliable or a test error).
* **Benefit:** replaces "pick the maximum" by "pick the stable plateau", the book's central message and the same direction as book 3 (best-of-N) and Chan (parameter sensitivity). **Complexity:** low–medium (analytics + endpoint + view). **Risks:** needs a regular grid; random or irregular sweeps need a different neighbour definition (use the n-space radius method, C19); do not present the adjusted return as a probability when returns are not normal. **Validation:** synthetic maps with a known plateau and a known spike; the spike is flagged; neighbour averages match hand-computed values at edges and corners.

### O2 — Pre-declared ranges, geometric grids and sweep ledger *(book: C3, C4, C16, C20, pp. 908–909, 941, 945)*
* A helper to build **geometric/percentage-step grids** (×1.5 or log-spaced), the default for period parameters; a stored **sweep specification** (strategy, parameter ranges, spacing, date range, asset, costs) created *before* running and not editable after results exist; a **ledger of total runs per strategy family and per asset/time range** so the number of trials is known (the book: "it is the cumulative number of tests across all strategies that should be considered"). Display "N tests so far" next to any reported best result and feed it to best-of-N/deflation methods (book 3 and book 5).
* **Complexity:** low–medium. **Risks:** a ledger can be gamed by creating new strategy names; document that it is an honesty aid. **Validation:** ranges immutable after the first result; counters increase by the number of runs.

### O3 — Locked final segment and chronological block rules *(book: C7, C8, pp. 916–919)*
Extend the split to **train / validation / locked** (the book also uses 70/20/10 for machine learning); the locked segment is evaluated at most once per configuration family and the API records that it was consumed (the book's "no second chance", "feedback"). Evaluate blocks by **daily (per-candle) returns**, not by total profit, when blocks are not contiguous. Alternating random blocks (fixed at the start of development and per system, so different systems do not share the same hold-out) is a more advanced variant; low priority. Same as book 3 ch. 6 O5. **Complexity:** medium. **Validation:** attempting a second look at the locked segment is recorded and shown; metrics unchanged for existing flows.

### O4 — Step-forward (walk-forward) analysis with short-term-bias diagnostics *(book: C8, pp. 918–919)*
Roll windows (for example 2 years in-sample, 6 months out) to choose parameters in-sample and accumulate only out-of-sample trades; report **parameter drift** across windows (the book's sign of too-short windows: optimum jumping from 10 to 50 to 15) and the stitched out-of-sample equity. Crypto note (ours): histories are short (Binance spot since 2017 for BTC; later for others), so a 2-year window leaves few windows; use 4h/1d carefully and expect wide uncertainty. **Complexity:** high (computation: each window is a sweep; reuse the sweep engine and keep `MAX_SWEEP_RUNS` per window). **Validation:** concatenated out-of-sample segments never overlap training windows; deterministic with the same seed.

### O5 — Shock-adjusted backtest report *(book: C26, C27, pp. 970–975)*
* **Proposal:** (a) detect "shock candles" by `range ≥ k × ATR(10)` (the book uses 5× for removal and 3.5× to list); (b) report net result **without** shock candles' P/L, and **with the sign of the average shock-day P/L reversed** for the days when the strategy profited; (c) count wins vs losses on shock days and their sizes; (d) flag runs where more than 50% of shocks were profits ("profit and risk are both wrong"); (e) in sweeps, add "profit excluding shocks" and "number of shock days held" columns, and use the **largest neighbour drawdown**, not the best run's, for risk (C26).
* **Why:** crypto has frequent shocks (liquidation cascades, exchange events). A long-only spot bot profits from upside shocks and suffers in downside shocks; an optimiser can pick a parameter set that happened to be flat or short before a crash.
* **Complexity:** medium (per-candle attribution of P/L in the engine; needs equity by candle). **Risks:** attribution when holding through multiple days; the book's reversal heuristic assumes symmetry, which is false for long-only spot (shocks of both signs are possible but long-only exposure is one-sided); state the assumption. **Validation:** synthetic series with a planted shock; attribution sums to total P/L.

### O6 — Cost ladder and break-even cost *(book: C17, C22, pp. 942, 949)*
Run the selected configuration at several cost levels (for example 0, 0.5×, 1×, 2×, 3× of assumed fee + slippage) and report the net result for each plus the **break-even cost multiple**; in sweeps show the profitable region shrinking with cost (the book's Figure 21.14). Also report **average net P/L per trade as a multiple of the round-turn cost** (book 4 ch. 16 O4). Remember that suspiciously high results *with zero costs* mean fast strategies and short-lived shocks. **Complexity:** low–medium. **Validation:** the 1× run equals the normal run; results are monotone in cost.

### O7 — Additional performance criteria *(book: C17, pp. 941–942)*
Add to `Metrics` or a separate analytics report: **time to recovery** (and longest drawdown duration), **time in market**, **slope of periodic (monthly/quarterly) returns** (decay detector), **average maximum drawdown across tests**, **profit excluding the best N trades / best month** (outlier review, C18), and **capital suggested = k × maximum drawdown** (book: three times) only as information. Same set as ch. 2 O1/O2; here the book adds decay slope and outlier reviews. **Complexity:** low. **Validation:** fixtures.

### O8 — Neighbour-aware parameter selection ("select the plateau") *(book: C9, C10, C15, pp. 919–929, 939–940)*
In the UI and the API's "recommended parameters", rank by the **neighbourhood-smoothed score** rather than the raw score, and show the top-k plateau centres. Combined with O1. The book also advises limiting parameters (four or fewer) and prefers the **slow side** of a two-plateau map for cost reasons. **Complexity:** low once O1 exists.

### O9 — Parameter-drift tracking and "model broken?" monitor *(book: C24, pp. 951–953)*
(a) Store the best/selected parameters for each retest of the same strategy over time and report the shift (the book retests after 5–10% new data; a large shift is a warning); (b) for a live/paper bot compare **current drawdown size and duration with the backtest's distribution** (the book: steady losses up to the previous maximum drawdown means something is wrong; models should not decay from day one) and raise an **alert** (needs the Notifier from earlier proposals). The drawdown probability formula `(1 − P)^(2μ/σ²)` can be used as a rough prior only (normal returns). **Complexity:** medium. **Risks:** false alarms in normal drawdown; fat tails. **Validation:** fixtures with known μ and σ; alert fires once per breach.

### O10 — Cross-asset consistency view *(book: C20, C25, pp. 945, 954)*
Add a "same rules, many assets" run (BTC, ETH, SOL, BNB…) and report the percentage of assets profitable and the dispersion. A strategy profitable in BTC and unprofitable in highly correlated ETH is suspect (the book's S&P vs NASDAQ example). Caveat: crypto assets are strongly correlated (one factor), so agreement overstates independence; and the platform's design is one bot per asset, so this is a research report, not a trading feature. **Complexity:** medium (loops over the existing backtest service; needs imported data for each). **Validation:** aggregates recompute from stored results.

### O11 — Fade-the-fast-trend variant as a structural hypothesis *(book: C23, pp. 950–951)*
Within a slow uptrend, enter on a turn of a fast average (pullback entry) and exit only on the slow trend turning. In the book the profit per trade rose (277 → 741) and trades fell (212 → 72) on one market. For crypto this is the pullback entry already listed in ch. 9 O1 and ch. 19; keep one hypothesis (**H-PULLBACK-IN-TREND**) and do not duplicate. **Complexity:** low–medium as one more parameterised strategy.

### O12 — Search methods: random sampling instead of full grids; genetic only with discipline *(book: C9, C12, pp. 919–922, 932–936)*
If grids grow beyond `MAX_SWEEP_RUNS`: (1) prefer **random (Monte Carlo) sampling of the parameter space** with a recorded seed, counters to check uniform coverage, and repeated runs — this keeps the *distribution* of results (needed for O1) while a genetic algorithm returns only maxima; (2) if a genetic search is ever added, follow the book's recipe — multiple restarts, save the top-20 strings and check their clustering; (3) never use sequential optimisation as the sole method on interacting parameters (the book's 2-D example misses the peak). **Complexity:** medium. Not recommended now.

### O13 — Live-vs-backtest reconciliation of expectations *(book: C2, C24, pp. 907, 952–953)*
Record at bot creation the **expected** profile (win rate, trades per month, maximum drawdown, average hold) taken from the chosen backtest, and compare live/paper reality with it on a schedule; this operationalises "set expectations before testing" and "is the model broken?". Overlaps with the paper-vs-backtest parity proposal (book 1/3). **Complexity:** medium.

### Not applicable
Futures-specific data series (back-adjusted, indexed, constant-forward), roll dates, cash data, Excel/TradeStation procedures, Futures Truth, synthetic data by kernel regression (not now; the fraction-same-sign idea only if synthetic data ever becomes necessary), steepest-descent optimisation on continuous surfaces, hedge-fund benchmark indexes.

## 7. Acceptance criteria and tests
* Aggregates and neighbour averages equal hand-computed values on synthetic maps including edges and corners; spikes flagged as peak solutions, plateaus not.
* Geometric grid helper yields strictly increasing integers without duplicates for period parameters.
* Locked segment evaluated once; later requests are refused or flagged; existing endpoints unchanged by default.
* Shock attribution sums to total P/L; shock-free run recomputes consistently.
* Cost ladder is monotone and its 1× point equals the baseline.
* ruff, mypy, existing tests green.

## Caveats
Most numeric examples are futures or US index results with $-denominated costs and slippage statements (S&P 50 basis points, fixed income, energy) that do not apply to crypto spot. The "adjusted return = mean − 1σ → 84%" and the drawdown probability assume normal returns.
